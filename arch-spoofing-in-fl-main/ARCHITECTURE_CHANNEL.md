# The architecture channel: spoofing model heterogeneity

**The second declared-metadata channel, written up to the same standard as Part A.**

> **What this is.** In model-heterogeneous Clustered Federated Learning the server
> groups clients by the **architecture they declare**, `metadata['arch']`, and trains one
> model per architecture. That field is unverified. This is the study of what happens when
> a client lies about it.
>
> **The lecture.** [`ARCHITECTURE_WALKTHROUGH.md`](ARCHITECTURE_WALKTHROUGH.md) explains
> every metric below from scratch and gives each number a verdict.
>
> **The notebook.** [`cfl-architecture.ipynb`](cfl-architecture.ipynb) recomputes every
> figure below from its source file, and is generated by
> [`notebooks/gen_arch_nb.py`](notebooks/gen_arch_nb.py) -- edit the generator, never the
> notebook.
>
> **Companion documents.** [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md) covers the *data* channel, Part A
> distribution shift and Part B concept shift. [`verify_arch_channel.py`](verify_arch_channel.py)
> reproduces the spine result below from the saved corpus.

## The two channels, and why this one is not "Part C"

The project attacks **one trust boundary through two channels**. They are different axes,
not a sequence:

| channel | server groups on | what differs between groups | attack |
|---|---|---|---|
| **architecture** | `metadata['arch']` | the **model** each client runs | declare a model you do not run |
| **data distribution** | `metadata['label_hist']` | the **data** each client holds | imitate a distribution you do not have |

`RESULTS.md` Parts A and B are two kinds of *data* heterogeneity, tested separately so
they can replicate one another. This document is the *model* axis. It came **first**: the
repository is named `arch-spoofing-in-fl` for it.

## Provenance, stated up front

These results come from `old_code/arch-spoofing-analysis.ipynb`, run 10 Aug 2026, corpus

That folder carries a "do not cite" notice. **It was written too broadly and has been

4 and 5 of that notebook are reinstated; section 3, which was the *distribution* half, stays
retired and has been redone properly as Part A.

**Reproduced on 30 Aug 2026**: `python verify_arch_channel.py` recomputes the spine from
the corpus using the shape-agnostic feature list that still lives in the live tree, and
asserts every figure against what the notebook printed. All pass.

**Two caveats that do carry over**, stated before any number:

- **Three seeds**, not five. The notebook's "6" is seeds times datasets.
- **Clients hold Dirichlet-partitioned data.** That does not affect who is in which
  cluster, because clusters are declared, but the absolute accuracies in M4 sit on a
  partition the project has since moved away from. **Quote the degradations, which are
  relative, not the absolute accuracies.**

## M1. Can behaviour identify an architecture at all?

Before asking whether fingerprinting defeats a spoofer, establish whether it can tell
architectures apart when nobody is lying. **Honest clients only, no attack, no payload.**

![Feature distributions](docs/figures/s1_feature_distributions.png)

A meta-classifier trained to predict the architecture from probe behaviour:

| dataset | clients | architectures | accuracy | majority baseline | lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IDS | 108 | 3 | **0.806** | 0.333 | **+0.472** |
| MNIST | 108 | 4 | **0.509** | 0.333 | +0.176 |

![Confusion](docs/figures/s1_confusion.png)

So architectures **do** leave a behavioural signature, strongly on IDS and weakly on
MNIST. The most informative feature by a wide margin is `pred_dist_gini` at **0.1861**,
the concentration of the model's predicted-class distribution: architectures differ most
in *how peaked their outputs are*, not in how accurate they are.

> **Verdict.** Better for the **server**, and it is the precondition everything else
> needs. If architectures were behaviourally identical, no fingerprint could exist and no
> later number would mean anything.

### The trap in this section, and it is the important part

Any feature derived from parameter or layer count makes this task trivial **and
meaningless**:

| feature set | IDS | MNIST |
|---|---|---|
| shape-agnostic (the headline) | 0.806 | 0.509 |
| plus shape-dependent | **1.000** | **1.000** |
| **`n_params` alone** | **1.000** | **1.000** |

![Shape leak](docs/figures/s1_shape_leak.png)

**Parameter count alone identifies the architecture perfectly, and tells the server
nothing it did not already know.** The declared architecture already implies the shape,
and an adaptive spoofer's shape matches by construction. So a fingerprint built on shape
is not a defence, it is a restatement of the declaration.

This is the architecture channel's version of Part A's full-vector trap: an apparently
perfect signal that is useless for the question actually being asked. **The headline
classifier therefore uses shape-agnostic features only**, and every detection number below
depends on that choice.

## M2 and M3. The imitation, and what bounds it

Two tiers of spoof, and the difference between them is the whole attack.

| tier | what it declares | what it trains | outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **naive** | a different architecture | **its own** | shape mismatch |
| **adaptive** | a different architecture | **the one it declared** | shape-valid |

**The bound on imitation is capability, not knowledge.** To spoof adaptively you must
actually be able to *run* the target architecture. That is the architecture analogue of
Part A's resampling bound, where the attacker could not manufacture a class it did not
hold. The difference is that architectures are public and small in number, so in practice
this bound barely binds. More on that in M4.

![Detection by mechanism](docs/figures/s2_detection_by_mechanism.png)

Three detection mechanisms, **reported separately and never pooled**, because conflating
them is how a defence gets credited with work it did not do:

| condition | payload | shape check | non-finite | fingerprint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| naive spoof | none | **1.000** | 0.000 | 0.972 |
| naive spoof | boost | **1.000** | 0.722 | 0.250 |
| naive spoof | sign flip | **1.000** | 1.000 | n/a |
| adaptive spoof | none | **0.000** | 0.000 | **0.250** |
| adaptive spoof | boost | **0.000** | 0.000 | 0.472 |
| adaptive spoof | sign flip | **0.000** | 0.000 | 0.917 |

**Honest false-alarm rate, out-of-sample: 0.343.**

**The naive spoof is caught for free.** The weight-shape check stops it 100% of the time,
before fingerprinting gets a say. A metadata-only lie is already defeated by basic shape
validation, which is exactly why the interesting case is the adaptive one.

**The adaptive spoof is invisible to that check, by construction.** Its update genuinely is
the architecture it claims, so there is no mismatch to find. Shape check 0.000 on every
adaptive condition.

> **Verdict.** **Better for the attack, worse for the server.** The free defence catches
> only the unsophisticated version. Against an attacker willing to train the model it
> declares, shape validation contributes nothing.

## M4. What must the attacker know? The inversion of Part A

This is where the architecture channel and the data channel come apart, and it is the
sharpest thing in this document.

**Part A's binding limitation is knowledge.** The attacker cannot work out the target
group's label distribution: it is continuous, high-dimensional, and the broadcast model
leaks it at only a couple of percentage points per class. Measured, `inferred` scored
0.0844 against `blind` 0.0887 with overlapping intervals, which is **not
distinguishable**. An attacker told the answer imitates perfectly; one that must deduce it
cannot.

**Architecture has none of those properties.**

| | data distribution | architecture |
|---|---|---|
| the thing to be guessed | a continuous vector over classes | **one name from a short list** |
| size of the space | effectively unbounded | **5 here, 7 in the live registry** |
| is it public? | no, it is the victim's private case mix | **yes, architectures are published** |
| can it be read off what the server sends? | barely, a two-point leak | **yes, parameter shapes give it away** |
| **binding constraint** | **knowledge** | **capability** |

M1's shape-leak table is the evidence for the last row: `n_params` alone identifies the
architecture at **1.000**. The same fact that makes shape-based fingerprinting useless as
a *defence* makes architecture trivially *knowable* to an attacker.

> **Verdict.** **Better for the attack than the data channel is, and the two fail for
> opposite reasons.** Distribution spoofing is bounded by knowledge and free in
> capability. Architecture spoofing is bounded by capability and free in knowledge. An
> attacker who can afford to train a second model faces no knowledge barrier at all.

That contrast is what makes this channel worth reporting beside Part A rather than as a
repetition of it.

## M5. Does the fingerprint defence catch the adaptive spoof?

**No.** This is the strongest negative result in the project.

| condition | fingerprint flags it | honest false-alarm rate | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| adaptive spoof, **no payload** | **0.250** | 0.343 | **below noise** |
| adaptive spoof, boost | 0.472 | 0.343 | not clearly above noise |
| adaptive spoof, sign flip | **0.917** | 0.343 | real, but see below |

**The adaptive spoofer with no payload is flagged less often than an innocent client.**
0.250 against a 0.343 false-alarm rate. A detection rate at or below the false-alarm rate
is the classifier's error rate, not a detection.

**Where detection does happen, it is finding the payload, not the spoof.** Sign-flipping
scores 0.917, but sign-flipping a BatchNorm network produces negative variances and hence
non-finite output, which any verifier catches trivially. The corpus records
`nonfinite_rate` precisely so this cannot be mistaken for fingerprinting, and the
architecture pool deliberately includes `mlp_plain`, a BatchNorm-free network, as the
control for it.

**Does the finding survive on MNIST alone?** The live re-run is MNIST-only, so it is fair
to ask whether the Intrusion Detection System (IDS) half is carrying this result. It is not.
Split by dataset, the spine sits below its own false-alarm rate in **both**:

| corpus slice | adaptive spoof, no payload | honest false-alarm rate | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| both datasets (quoted above) | 0.250 | 0.343 | below noise |
| **MNIST only** | **0.389** | **0.491** | **below noise** |
| IDS only | 0.111 | 0.194 | below noise |

The exact rates move, the conclusion does not. Pooling the two datasets tightens the
estimate; it does not create the finding. **The caveat that does bite is the partition,
not the dataset**: all 1512 rows are Dirichlet-partitioned, which is why the degradations
rather than the absolute accuracies are the transferable numbers.

**The method is out-of-sample and that mattered.** Detection is leave-one-seed-out: train
on honest clients from every seed but one, score the held-out seed. The notebook records
why, and it is worth quoting because it is the same trap Part A's trap list warns about:

> "An earlier version fitted on honest rows and scored those same rows, which put the
> false-alarm rate at 0.000. A random forest has no training error, so that figure was an
> artefact, and it made a 27% detection rate look like signal."

> **Verdict.** **Better for the attack, worse for the server, and it closes a defence
> direction.** An attacker that infiltrates and delivers nothing, or delivers a boost that
> keeps the update's direction honest, passes every check the server has. Behavioural
> fingerprinting cannot separate a well-executed architecture spoof from an honest client.

## M6. What the spoof actually costs the victim

The attack run end to end through the real server, pooled over seeds with bootstrap
intervals.

![Impact versus alpha](docs/figures/s4_impact_vs_alpha.png)

| Dirichlet alpha | baseline | under attack | degradation | relative drop |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.4917 | 0.1692 | 0.3225 [0.248, 0.470] | **65%** |
| 0.5 | 0.8342 | 0.1667 | 0.6675 [0.485, 0.762] | **79%** |
| 10.0 | 0.9950 | 0.2575 | 0.7375 [0.735, 0.740] | **74%** |

Effect sizes are large at every setting: Cohen's d of **2.52**, **4.22**, and one so large
the standard deviation collapses.

![Effect sizes](docs/figures/s5_forest.png)

**And the effect is the treatment, not the seed.** Decomposing the variance in
victim-cluster accuracy:

| factor | share of variance |
|---|---|
| **condition** | **0.759** |
| alpha | 0.135 |
| **seed** | **0.003** |
| residual | 0.103 |

![Variance decomposition](docs/figures/s5_variance.png)

Seed explains **0.3%**. On a three-seed study that is the number that makes the result
believable, because the obvious objection to three seeds is that the effect could be
sampling noise, and here it demonstrably is not.

> **Verdict.** **Better for the attack, badly worse for the server's honest clients.** The
> victim cluster loses between two-thirds and four-fifths of its accuracy, at every data
> setting tested.

## M7 and M8. The live re-run on the current Torch pipeline

Everything above comes from the archived corpus. `TorchFederatedServer` has since been
ported to multiple architectures, so the channel now runs under the **same schema, guard
and device as Part A**. Two experiments have been run on it.

**Setup for both:** MNIST, clustered partition, 12 clients round-robin over `mlp_flat`
(110,156 parameters) and `dnn_flat` (578,894), 6 clients each; attacker is client 0,
home `mlp_flat`, declaring `dnn_flat`; 6 rounds of 2 epochs; seeds 0, 1, 2.

### M7. The two tiers, and what the shape check catches

*`python sweeps_arch.py tiers` · [`experiments/m1_spoof_tiers/`](experiments/m1_spoof_tiers/)*

![Spoof tiers](docs/figures/arch_1_tiers.png)

| condition | infiltration | rounds rejected | victim accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| control, no spoof | **0.000** | 0 | 0.9157 |
| naive spoof | **1.000** | **6 of 6** | **0.9157** |
| adaptive spoof | **1.000** | **0 of 6** | 0.9250 |

**Both tiers are placed**, because placement reads only the declared string. **The naive
tier is then rejected totally**: its victim accuracy equals the control's to six decimal
places on every individual seed (0.9330, 0.8975, 0.9165), which is the proof that rejection
is complete rather than merely frequent.

**And placement alone does no damage.** The adaptive spoofer sits +0.0093 *above* control.
An infiltrator that trains properly on real data is, in accuracy terms, an extra
contributor. **Placement is the precondition for harm, not harm itself** — which is why M8
attaches a payload.

> **Verdict.** **Better for the attack, and it narrows the defence question.** Shape
> validation is free and perfect against the tier that was never the threat, and worth
> nothing against the tier that is.

### M8. Six aggregation rules, with a payload attached

*`python sweeps_arch.py agg` · [`experiments/m3_aggregators/`](experiments/m3_aggregators/)*

The attacker carries a **10× weight boost** (model replacement) composed onto the spoof.

![Aggregation](docs/figures/arch_3_aggregation.png)

| aggregator | placement | control | victim, attacked | victim, control | accuracy lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **fedavg** | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.7350 | 0.9157 | **+0.1807** |
| krum | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.7968 | 0.7938 | −0.0030 |
| trimmed | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.9303 | 0.9257 | −0.0047 |
| median | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.9320 | 0.9267 | −0.0053 |
| multikrum | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.9157 | 0.9093 | −0.0063 |
| bulyan | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.9207 | 0.9093 | −0.0113 |

**Placement is 1.000 under every rule**, and could not be otherwise: it is decided before
aggregation runs. **FedAvg loses 18.1 points**, consistently (+0.1910, +0.1365, +0.2145
across seeds). **The five robust rules lose nothing measurable.**

**This replicates Part A's correction on a structurally different channel**, which is the
strongest form the claim has taken.

**One caveat the table makes visible.** Krum's *control* is 0.7938 against FedAvg's 0.9157
— it pays 12.2 points of clean accuracy to select a single update. Median and trimmed mean
contain the damage *and* keep the accuracy.

> **Verdict.** **Better for the server on damage, no help at all on membership.**
> Containment is not eviction: the spoofer is still inside the victim's cluster every
> round, and free to switch to a payload built to survive coordinate-wise filtering.

**Two failures in M8 worth recording**, both of which produced plausible numbers rather
than errors. **The payload initially never fired** — `CompositeAttack` had no
`shape_update`, so the boost hit a base-class no-op while every row recorded
`payload=boost`. **The fix then diverged via BatchNorm**: boosting the flat vector scales
`running_var` (1,924 of 578,894 coordinates) negative, the forward pass square-roots it,
and the model emits NaN. Victim accuracy read 0.0000 at a boost of just **1.2×**, which is
what exposed it. `WeightBoost` now amplifies **learnable parameters only**, per Bagdasaryan
et al. The NaN artefact is the same trap as the sign-flip detection rate in M5.

## What this establishes

1. **Architectures are behaviourally identifiable**, but only weakly once shape-derived
   features are excluded: 0.806 on IDS, 0.509 on MNIST, against a 0.333 baseline.
2. **Shape-based fingerprinting is circular.** `n_params` alone scores 1.000 and tells the
   server nothing it did not already know from the declaration.
3. **The naive spoof is free to catch.** Shape validation stops it 100% of the time.
4. **The adaptive spoof is invisible.** Flagged 0.250 against a 0.343 false-alarm rate,
   which is **less often than an innocent client**.
5. **Detection tracks the payload, not the spoof.** The one high number, 0.917, is
   catching non-finite output from a wrecked model.
6. **The damage is severe and is not seed noise.** 65 to 79% of victim-cluster accuracy,
   with condition explaining 75.9% of variance and seed 0.3%.
7. **The binding constraint is capability, not knowledge**, which is the exact inverse of
   the data channel.

## What this does not establish

- **Three seeds and two datasets.** Enough for the variance decomposition to be
  reassuring, not enough to claim a rate precisely.
- **No detector was built for the payload**, which is what the numbers suggest would
  actually work. Detection here is a by-product of a fingerprint aimed at something else.
- **Clients hold Dirichlet-partitioned data**, so absolute accuracies sit on a partition
  the project has moved away from. The degradations are relative and unaffected.
- **The `mlp_plain` control exists but is not swept.** BatchNorm's role in the sign-flip
  result is argued from one architecture rather than measured across many.
- **Sections M1 to M6 have not been rerun on the current Torch pipeline.** The port now
  exists and M7 and M8 use it, but the fingerprinting and victim-impact numbers above still
  come from the TensorFlow corpus. **No single run produces both bodies of evidence**, and
  they should not be quoted as though it did.
- **M8 tests one payload at one scale.** A 10× weight boost is the classic model
  replacement attack. Whether the robust rules contain a payload *designed* to survive
  coordinate-wise filtering — small, spread over many rounds, within honest variance — is
  untested.
- **The live re-run has only two architectures, both dense**, and the shape check works
  only because their parameter counts differ. **Two architectures with equal parameter
  counts are easy to arrange deliberately and that case is untested**; it is the one
  experiment that could overturn "the naive spoof is free to catch".

## Questions a supervisor is likely to ask

> **"This is in a folder marked do-not-cite. Why are you showing it to me?"**
>
> Because that notice was too broad and has been corrected in place. It retires work
> measuring infiltration into a Dirichlet-derived grouping. Architecture clusters come
> from a declared field, so nothing here recovers a Dirichlet grouping. One of the
> notebook's five sections was affected, the distribution half, and that has been redone
> properly as Part A. `verify_arch_channel.py` recomputes the spine result and asserts it
> against the original figures; it reproduces exactly.

> **"A 25% detection rate still sounds like some detection."**
>
> It is below the false-alarm rate of 34.3% on honest clients, measured out-of-sample on
> the same rule. A rate at or below the false-alarm rate is the classifier's error rate.
> The attacker is flagged **less** often than an innocent client, which is worse for the
> defence than no detection at all, because acting on it would remove more honest clients
> than attackers.

> **"Three seeds is not many."**
>
> Agreed, and it is stated as a limitation. What makes it defensible here is the variance
> decomposition: condition explains 75.9% of the variance in victim-cluster accuracy and
> seed explains 0.3%. The usual objection to three seeds is that the effect might be
> sampling noise, and that is measurable here and ruled out.

> **"Why is your headline classifier worse than one using all the features?"**
>
> Because the better one is cheating. Parameter count alone gives 1.000, and the server
> already knows the declared parameter count. A fingerprint built on shape restates the
> declaration rather than checking it, and an adaptive spoofer matches the shape by
> construction. The weaker number is the honest one.

> **"How is this different from the distribution attack?"**
>
> They fail for opposite reasons. Distribution spoofing is bounded by **knowledge**: the
> attacker cannot work out the target's label mix, and `inferred` was not distinguishable
> from guessing uniform. Architecture spoofing is bounded by **capability**: you must be
> able to train the model you claim, but there is no knowledge barrier, because
> architectures are public and parameter shapes give them away.
